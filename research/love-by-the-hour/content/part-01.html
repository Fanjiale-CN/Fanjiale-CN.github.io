Abstract

China’s paid gaming-companion market sells more than help with winning games. In the materials analyzed here, payment can also purchase recognition, priority, private access, and an expectation of preferential alignment: someone who is more likely to answer first, protect the client in conflict, or remain available across memberships and private groups. This paper asks how such paid closeness is produced, how it moves across public and private channels, and what happens when a customer becomes a friend, member, promoter, or defender of the creator. Using an exploratory design centered on one focal streamer ecology and 18 purposively selected public comparator accounts, the study analyzes 47 coded field-evidence entries, three focal interaction episodes, membership and gifting interfaces, group announcements, private-domain routing, organizational contacts, and one supplementary consumer interview. The focal case is the only account under continuing opportunity-based live observation; comparator accounts are used to map visible business models rather than infer private motives or population prevalence. I propose “paid partiality” as a sensitizing concept for the commercially produced expectation that a provider will preferentially affirm, protect, prioritize, or emotionally align with a paying customer. The evidence identifies four linked mechanisms: platform tiers convert attention into differential access; public content routes users toward persistent private channels; studios, guilds, managers, and customer-service accounts help scale an apparently personal relationship; and creator communities can turn consumers into moderators, promoters, or defenders. The paper argues that the ecology documented here prices not only play, but structured proximity, alignment, and belonging.

Keywords: gaming companionship; emotional labor; paid intimacy; relational labor; fandom; China; livestreaming; platform work; paid partiality

1. Introduction: Someone on Your Side

Forty yuan bought one interviewee an hour of companionship.

The transaction began inside a game. She chose a companion, paid for a block of time, and entered a match. In the simplest version, the purchase is easy to describe: skill is rented. The companion should play well, raise the probability of winning, and make the hour less frustrating. Yet the interviewee described a more specific expectation. If other teammates criticized her, she said, the companion should be “on your side.” What she wanted was not only competence. It was “a kind of companionship that belongs to one person,” and, in her own word, “favoritism.” [Field evidence E021.]

That word changes the object of analysis.

A win can be counted. A rank can be displayed. A high-scoring jungler can prove technical competence with match statistics. Favoritism is harder to measure, but it can still be produced through observable practices: protecting a client from criticism, remembering a nickname, answering a member before a stranger, placing a familiar customer in a private group, changing register after a gift, or creating a sense that one relationship receives access unavailable to everyone else. Around these gestures sits visible commercial machinery: membership tiers, livestream gifts, private contacts, studios, guilds, customer-service accounts, order groups, “follow-car” services, one-to-one access, voice performance, and relationship role-play.

The unusual thing is that these systems do not simply attach money to a preexisting relationship. They help manufacture the conditions under which a relationship can be felt.

This is why gaming companionship cannot be adequately understood as a narrow service category. It belongs to a wider family of markets in which the commodity is partly relational. Egan (2005) called attention to “emotional consumption” in service encounters where economic exchange begins to merge with a sense of relationality. Zelizer (2000) showed that money and intimacy do not occupy naturally separate worlds; people routinely create monetary arrangements inside relationships that remain emotionally meaningful. Hochschild’s (1983) classic account of emotional labor explains how workers manage displayed feeling as part of paid service, while Baym (2015) extends the problem across time through the idea of relational labor: the ongoing work of building and maintaining ties that support future economic activity.

China’s game-companion sector provides an unusually concentrated setting in which these processes can be observed together. Shen et al. (2021), studying dedicated Bixin users, found that customers often wanted paid teammates to interact “like friends,” that communication frequently moved to WeChat, and that genuine friendship remained comparatively rare. Jiao (2022) documented companions protecting clients in games, responding to emotional needs, chatting, and maintaining long-term customer relationships. Zhao (2023) showed that Chinese platform game work depends not only on digital platforms but also on extra-platform intermediaries such as studios and guilds, as well as relationship labor used in order matching and community management. Recent work has pushed further into the gendered production of voice and feeling. Chen and Chen (2025) describe female companions’ voices as marketable assets used to construct gendered personas; Yuan and Shao (2026) show how female companions perform emotional labor under algorithmic management and commercial feeling rules. Mo et al. (2026) identify an “order-bound” mechanism in which immersive emotional involvement during paid sessions is followed by deliberate disengagement after the order.

This literature has made the labor side of the market substantially more visible. We know much more about how companionship is performed, organized, and bounded. We know less about what happens after personalized attention has successfully produced familiarity, attachment, or belonging.

The consumer side is beginning to receive more attention. Huo, Wan Abas, and Waheed (2026), using a survey of 425 users, found that professionalism, interactivity, and popularity shape perceived entertainment and sociability, which in turn influence intention to use gaming-companion services. Livestreaming research in China similarly shows that intimacy can be economically consequential. Cao et al. (2022) found that verbal intimacy and virtual physical intimacy were associated with users’ willingness to move toward more expensive virtual gifts. Zhang and Liu (2024) found that streamer attractiveness, expertise, and parasocial interaction were positively associated with gift-giving intention among Chinese livestream users. These studies are important, but they usually stop at adoption, purchase intention, or gifting. The more difficult question comes later: when repeated spending produces familiarity, when familiarity produces attachment, and when attachment becomes a social identity.

The distinction matters because game companionship is unusually interactive. Classic parasocial relationships are structurally asymmetric: viewers feel that they know a media figure who may not know them. Gaming companions, by contrast, may know the customer’s name, hear the customer’s voice, coordinate in real time, move to WeChat, remember previous matches, and potentially become genuine friends. Wang’s (2025) study of intimacy in Honor of Kings demonstrates that the game itself is already an “affective infrastructure.” Voice chat, repeated co-presence, synchronized action, rescue, joint victories, and shared pressure can generate real closeness between strangers even without a paid service. Money enters a setting that is already relationally productive.

This paper therefore asks a different set of questions. What exactly is being purchased when a customer pays for a gaming companion? How are attention, intimacy, and preferential alignment produced across paid sessions, livestreams, memberships, and private groups? When do commercial relationships remain bounded by the order, and when do they cross into reciprocal friendship? When does a customer begin to act like a fan or a community member? And what happens when the person being defended behaves in ways that conflict with the persona around which the relationship was built?

A focal case makes the tension visible. The streamer analyzed here, anonymized as Streamer W, publicly combines game competence with soft-spoken interaction, family-oriented posts, and a broader persona of warmth. In one archived livestream sequence, however, he spent several minutes insulting other teammates with escalating language, including death-directed internet abuse. Seconds after a viewer sent a virtual gift, his delivery changed. He thanked the viewer in a markedly softer voice and used an affectionate nickname. The point is not to uncover his “real personality.” Two emotional registers were publicly observable in the same commercial environment, and the shift occurred when a monetized interaction became salient. [Field evidence E001–E005.]

A later livestream supplied a second puzzle. When a viewer questioned the situation in the room, a moderator answered defensively, another ordinary viewer joined in, and the critic was eventually removed. [Field evidence E010–E013.] In a group associated with the streamer, members were asked to help a new video by pressing free likes and sending free promotional tokens because its traffic was weak. In another post, viewers who felt a strong sense of “immersion” after watching the streamer’s videos were directed toward private contacts and an affiliated club, from which managers could place them into order groups. [Field evidence E025–E027.]

The sequence is easy to miss if each component is studied separately. Content creates attraction. Interaction creates familiarity. Membership prices access. Private groups lengthen contact. Clubs process orders. Moderators enforce boundaries. Viewers help with traffic. A person who arrived as an audience member can end up as a customer, a member, a volunteer promoter, or a defender.

This article calls that broader movement the social life of paid intimacy. Its central claim is deliberately bounded: across the ecology documented here, the paid product can extend beyond time with another player into structured proximity — being recognized, prioritized, routed into smaller spaces, or positioned as someone whose interests receive special weight. The most revealing commodity may be the one that looks least like a commodity: partiality.
