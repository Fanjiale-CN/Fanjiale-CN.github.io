2. From Emotional Labor to Paid Intimacy

2.1 Emotional consumption: buying an experience that can answer back

Most commodities do not know who bought them. Service relationships do.

That difference is the starting point for Egan’s (2005) concept of emotional consumption. Studying regular customers in an exotic dance club, Egan argued that consuming service labor differs from consuming objects because the transaction unfolds between subjects. The consumer does not merely receive an output; he or she encounters a person whose attention, affect, and responsiveness become part of the product. Under those conditions, the boundary between economic exchange and relationality can blur.

Gaming companionship intensifies this dynamic because the service is built around synchronous interaction. The provider does not deliver a finished object. The provider reacts to the customer’s mistakes, tone, frustration, jokes, silences, victories, conflicts, and requests in real time. The service exists only while the relationship is being enacted. A customer can therefore evaluate the transaction through both instrumental outcomes (“Did we win?”) and relational outcomes (“Did I feel seen?”).

Huo et al. (2026) provide quantitative evidence for this duality. Their survey of 425 users who had used gaming-companion services distinguishes perceived entertainment from perceived sociability, and both contribute to intention to use. Yet “sociability” remains broad. It can describe pleasant conversation, a friend-like atmosphere, flirtation, or a provider who consistently privileges the client’s emotional position. The present study isolates one mechanism inside that broader category: partiality.

I use “paid partiality” as a sensitizing concept rather than a settled scale. It refers to a commercially produced expectation that a provider will preferentially affirm, protect, prioritize, or emotionally align with the paying customer. This can be expressed verbally, through gameplay, through queue priority, through access, or through community rules. It does not require the provider to lie. A companion may sincerely like the customer. Nor does it require that every customer want unconditional agreement. The analytical point is that preferential treatment itself can acquire exchange value.

Paid partiality sits beside, but is not identical to, customer service. A waiter may be attentive, but is rarely expected to defend the diner in a dispute with the next table. A tutor may be supportive, but the service is not normally advertised through a promise of emotional alignment. In game companionship, however, the product often exists inside conflict-rich environments: competitive matches, ranked games, public livestreams, and fan communities. “Being on my side” can therefore become an observable feature of service.

2.2 Emotional labor and the managed voice

If emotional consumption describes part of what the customer receives, emotional labor describes part of what the worker does.

Hochschild (1983) defined emotional labor as the management of feeling and emotional display for a wage. Later research distinguishes surface acting, in which workers alter outward display without necessarily changing internal feeling, from deep acting, in which they attempt to generate the expected feeling itself. A major meta-analysis by Hülsheger and Schewe (2011) found that the costs of emotional labor differ substantially by strategy: surface acting and emotional dissonance are more consistently linked to strain, while deep acting has a more complicated relationship with performance and well-being. This matters for gaming companionship because not every warm interaction should be treated as fake. Some providers may genuinely enjoy a session; some customers may become friends; some forms of deep engagement may be experienced as meaningful.

The Chinese game-companion literature has made this labor visible in concrete ways. Jiao (2022) documents companions helping and protecting clients during play, responding to emotional needs, chatting, managing accounts, and maintaining long-term relationships. Zhao (2023) shows that relationship labor is embedded in order matching and community management across a fragmented ecology of platforms, studios, guilds, and workers. Yuan and Shao (2026), based on 31 interviews with female game companions on Bixin, identify commercial feeling rules layered onto everyday and service-industry norms. Chen and Chen (2025), interviewing 15 female companions, show how vocal performance is itself commodified: timbre, gendered vocal styles, and imagined bodily qualities become part of the persona that customers buy.

Voice is especially important because it makes emotional display continuous. A profile can be edited before publication. A voice must react live. Tone, pitch, laughter, softness, pauses, irritation, and affection leak information even when words remain polite. Wang (2025) similarly finds voice central to the formation of intimacy among Honor of Kings players: real-time speech functions as both communication and embodied signal. The gaming-companion economy turns that already-affective medium into paid labor.

The focal case demonstrates why emotional labor cannot be reduced to generic “being nice.” Streamer W’s shift from sustained abusive speech toward other teammates to a gentle, personalized thank-you after a gift is analytically useful because the emotional display changed while the same person, platform, and livestream remained constant. What changed was the relationship made salient in the moment. [Field evidence E001–E005.] This is an observable display shift consistent with the commercial feeling rules described in prior work; it does not reveal the streamer’s private emotional state.

2.3 Relational labor: when the job continues after the order

Emotional labor can occur in a single encounter. Relational labor is built for repetition.

Baym (2015) uses relational labor to describe the ongoing work of building and maintaining relationships with audiences so that cultural work can remain economically sustainable. The concept is especially useful for creators because the customer relationship does not end when one unit of content is consumed. Musicians, influencers, streamers, and companions must remain available enough to be remembered, familiar enough to feel close, and distinct enough to keep the relationship valuable.

China’s gaming-companion ecology makes this continuation explicit. Shen et al. (2021) found that Bixin users frequently expected paid teammates to communicate as friends and that interaction often moved from the platform to WeChat. The migration matters. A platform order is time-bounded. WeChat is persistent. The worker can remain present between purchases, and the customer can continue to experience the relationship outside the formal service window.

The same logic appears in the material analyzed here. Streamer W’s ecosystem includes livestreams, membership spaces, private groups, an affiliated club, private-domain contacts, managers, and order groups. Other sampled accounts openly route users from Douyin profiles toward studios, customer-service contacts, guilds, “follow-car” services, one-to-one offerings, or private messaging. [Field evidence E025, E027, E030–E043.] In this sense, relational labor is infrastructural. The provider does not simply remember a customer; the business builds places where remembering can continue.

This also clarifies why the term “parasocial relationship” must be used carefully. Horton and Wohl’s classic formulation describes a structurally one-sided intimacy with a media persona. Livestreaming complicates that asymmetry, and gaming companionship complicates it further. A companion may know the customer by name, hear the customer’s voice, respond directly, enter private channels, and coordinate in synchronous play. Some relationships remain audience-like; some are reciprocal but commercial; some become genuine friendships. Dibble, Hartmann, and Rosaen’s (2016) distinction between parasocial interaction and parasocial relationships remains useful, but the present material also requires an analytic category for reciprocal paid intimacy.

2.4 Money inside real relationships

The common-sense story says that money contaminates intimacy. Zelizer’s (2000) work shows why this is analytically weak.

People routinely combine monetary transfers with intimate ties. Couples share bills. Parents pay children. Friends reimburse one another. Care work can be both loving and compensated. What matters is not whether money is present but how people differentiate relationships and assign appropriate monetary practices to them.

One interview in this project offers a compact example. A female university student described becoming genuinely close friends with a companion she had initially paid. The commercial origin of the relationship did not prevent friendship. Yet when she asked the friend to play during hours that could otherwise be used for paid orders, she continued to pay. Her reasoning was practical: the friend “earns money with her time,” and taking those working hours without paying would deprive her of income. The friendship remained real; the price attached to a particular category of time. [Field evidence E022.]

This interview case should not be treated as a refutation of Mo et al.’s (2026) “order-bound” model. Their 22 interviewees described immersive deep acting during paid sessions followed by deliberate disengagement after the order, a strategy that can protect workers from relational spillover. The current case instead functions as a deviant pathway: one tie crossed the order boundary and then renegotiated, rather than abolished, the price boundary. The more useful question is therefore not “Is the friendship real?” but “Which parts of a real relationship remain marketized?”

2.5 From customer to fan: the identity problem

A customer is defined by a transaction. A fan is defined by attachment. A community member is defined by belonging.

These positions can overlap, but they generate different behaviors. A customer may complain when service is poor. A fan may defend the creator. A community member may defend the group itself. This shift is where research on social identity becomes useful.

Tajfel and Turner’s social identity framework predicts that people derive part of the self from group memberships, making group boundaries psychologically consequential. Sanderson’s (2013) analysis of 717 Facebook posts by sports fans confronting an identity threat found patterns of rallying, stigmatization, victimization, intimidation, and degradation. More recent evidence points in the same direction. Forner et al. (2025), in a preregistered study of 265 Chinese and German pop-culture fans, found that stronger parasocial relationships were positively associated with in-group identification, out-group hostility, and behavioral aggression toward other fandoms. The Chinese subsample showed higher levels of all three outcomes. Chen, Teo, and Loh (2026), studying 500 sports fans on Sina Weibo, likewise found that fan identity was associated with cyberbullying behavior after personality factors were taken into account.

These findings do not establish that spending causes aggression. That claim would be far too simple. Viewers can spend heavily without becoming group defenders, and highly identified fans can contribute substantial unpaid labor without spending much. The useful insight is that economic, emotional, and social investment can accumulate in the same relationship. The customer is not necessarily transformed into a fan, but the infrastructure makes such a transformation possible.

The current study therefore treats the sequence “customer → fan → defender” as a pathway to investigate, not a universal lifecycle. It also pays attention to the opposite outcomes: criticism, disengagement, and exit. A theory that can only explain loyalty after controversy is a bad theory of fandom. People also leave.
