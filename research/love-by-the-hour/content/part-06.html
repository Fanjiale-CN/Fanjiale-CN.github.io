9. From Customer to Community Member

A transaction can end without creating a group. Membership changes that.

The sampled accounts repeatedly use groups, badges, membership tiers, fan levels, private spaces, and creator-specific communities to turn individual consumption into repeated social presence. Streamer W’s page displayed hundreds of member joins and multiple group chats; the membership interface offered member groups, member spaces, one-to-one questions, priority service, exclusive expressions, badges, and other privileges. [Field evidence E006, E014.] Other accounts ranged from a few dozen member joins to several thousand. Because Douyin’s “total joined” metric is not yet clear enough to interpret as an active-subscriber count, these cross-account figures are treated as platform-displayed participation indicators rather than conversion rates. [E042–E045, E047.]

Membership changes the unit of consumption. A gift is an event. A membership is a status.

Status can then become visible in the room through badges, levels, access, and recognition. It can also become a basis for group identity: the viewer is no longer only someone who watches the creator, but someone who belongs to a named space around the creator.

The Streamer W materials show two consequences of this change.

The first is governance. In the observed dispute, a moderator defended the streamer, an apparently ordinary viewer joined the defense, and the critic was removed. [E010–E013.] The moderator’s role matters because it converts support into formal room power: a supporter can help enforce the boundary of participation.

The second is promotional labor. In a group post, Streamer W asked members to help a newly posted video because traffic was poor. Members were encouraged to press free likes and send free promotional tokens. [E026.] This is observable audience effort directed toward the creator’s visibility.

The economic roles can therefore begin to reverse. The consumer originally gives money or attention to the creator; the creator later asks the consumer to contribute effort.

This is not necessarily exploitative in the moral sense. Fans often enjoy helping creators. Collective promotion can be part of participation and belonging. The key analytical point is that the economic roles have become entangled. The “customer” is now also a distributor, community member, moderator, advertiser, or defender.

Baym’s relational labor is helpful here, and the focal case suggests a possible reciprocal extension: creator relational labor can mobilize fan labor. The creator invests attention in the audience; the audience may later invest time, moderation, circulation, or promotional effort in the creator.

Fandom research supplies mechanisms that may make such effort socially meaningful. Forner et al. (2025), in a preregistered study of 265 Chinese and German pop-culture fans, found stronger parasocial relationships to be associated with stronger in-group identification, out-group hostility, and behavioral aggression toward other fandoms. Sanderson (2013) showed how sports fans used Facebook to collectively manage an identity threat through rallying, stigmatization, victimization, intimidation, and degradation. Chen, Teo, and Loh (2026), studying 500 sports fans on Sina Weibo, likewise found fan identity to be associated with cyberbullying behavior. These studies concern fandom rather than game-companion customers, so they provide mechanisms to test rather than direct explanations of the focal room.

The present case does not prove that these moderators or viewers behaved aggressively because they spent money. It does something more modest and more concrete: it shows the coexistence of monetized proximity, group identity, formal moderation power, ordinary-viewer defense, and unpaid promotional labor inside one creator-centered ecology.

That combination may alter how criticism is processed. A stranger evaluating a streamer can ask, “Did he do something wrong?” A group member may also ask what the criticism means for a valued community or for prior investments of time and status. When that second problem becomes salient, reputational conflict can become an identity conflict.

This framework offers one reason creator controversies can generate intense reactions around relatively ordinary creators. The economic relationship is only one layer; the creator can also become a node around which repeated recognition, group membership, and status are organized.

The phrase “our streamer” captures the shift.

The possessive pronoun is doing social work.

10. When Intimacy Becomes Labor

The worker-side risk of paid intimacy becomes clearest when the customer begins to treat payment as a claim over increasingly private forms of self-presentation.

The exploratory interview described sexual remarks, pressure for more suggestive interaction, and situations in which companions feared losing recurring customers if they refused higher-intimacy requests. Some observations were first-hand and some were reports about companions known to the interviewee, so they cannot establish prevalence. [Field evidence E023.] They do, however, identify boundary problems that also appear in the existing worker-side literature and in recent regulation.

Jiao (2022) documents gender violence and emotional pressure in gaming-companion labor. Chen and Chen (2025) show how female companions’ voices can be sexualized and marketed as embodied feminine personas, creating both economic opportunity and vulnerability. Yuan and Shao (2026) locate emotional labor within gendered commercial feeling rules and algorithmic management. Zhao (2025) likewise shows how platform game work channels women toward service-centered roles that are systematically devalued. Together, this literature makes clear that intimacy work is structured by gender as well as by customer demand.

The problem is structural because intimacy has no natural unit.

A game booster can define an outcome: reach a rank, complete a number of wins, finish an account task. Intimate service is harder to delimit. “Talk to me” can become “talk to me longer.” “Be nice to me” can become “flirt with me.” “Send me a voice message” can become a request for a more suggestive one. “We are close” can become a claim on private images. These examples describe a possible escalation structure, not a claim that every customer or service follows it.

Flirtation, role-play, or eroticized performance is not inherently coercive when adults voluntarily agree on the service. The analytic line is negotiated scope and the worker’s practical ability to refuse. Sexualized labor becomes a labor-rights problem when workers face disproportionate economic penalties for refusing escalation, when customers demand services outside the agreed exchange, or when harassment is normalized as the price of keeping a client.

Chinese regulators have also identified a specific risk around companionship language and sexualized chat. In July 2026, the Cyberspace Administration of China’s minors-protection campaign listed the use of “summer game companionship” language to lure minors into soft-pornographic chatting among the behaviors targeted for cleanup (Cyberspace Administration of China, 2026). The notice should not be generalized to the adult game-companion market. It does show that regulators recognize a route through which gaming, private conversation, and sexualized interaction can be bundled.

The difficulty for workers is that boundary defense may undermine the very persona they are paid to sell. A worker who must appear warm, available, affectionate, or romantically responsive may be penalized for suddenly becoming formal at the moment a customer crosses a line. The better the worker has been at producing closeness, the more entitled the customer may feel to continuity.

This is one possible dark edge of paid intimacy.

When a customer has purchased personalized access or the expectation of special treatment, refusal can be experienced as withdrawal. The worker may therefore have to manage not only feeling displays but the customer’s disappointment when the service reaches a boundary. The current evidence is insufficient to show that paid partiality itself causes sexual escalation; the stronger claim is that commercially produced closeness can make boundaries harder to negotiate.

That is why boundary creep belongs at the center of the analysis, not in a final paragraph about “risks.” The same mechanisms that make the product commercially powerful — personalized attention, exclusivity, familiarity, voice, private channels, repeated contact — are the mechanisms that make the worker’s limits difficult to stabilize.
