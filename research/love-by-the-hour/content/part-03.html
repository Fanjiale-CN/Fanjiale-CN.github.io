3. Research Design: One Deep Case, Eighteen Ecological Comparators

This project uses an exploratory digital-ethnographic case-study design combined with comparative platform-ecology analysis. It is designed to explain mechanisms rather than estimate population prevalence.

The research centers on one focal account, anonymized as Streamer W, and uses 18 additional accounts as public ecological comparators. The comparators span follower counts from a few thousand to several million and include technical streamers, companionship-oriented accounts, guild-linked micro-streamers, studio brands, teaching/member-service accounts, voice-performance organizations, and large gaming creators. They are not a random sample of Chinese game streamers or companions. They were selected purposively because their public profiles exposed different business infrastructures, membership systems, recruitment practices, service categories, or persona strategies.

As of this draft, the evidence registry contains 47 coded entries. Most are direct screenshots, platform interfaces, or transcribed public livestream material; interview-derived entries are separately labeled by evidentiary level. The project has reconstructed three interaction episodes for the focal case: a multi-minute sequence of abusive speech toward teammates, the immediate display shift after a gift, and a later dispute involving moderator defense, ordinary-viewer defense, and exclusion of a critic. The comparative archive includes profile pages, membership screens, gift menus, group announcements, private-domain routing, studio and guild contacts, order-group procedures, and public marketing language.

The unit of behavioral analysis is the interaction episode rather than the isolated sentence. Wherever possible, an episode preserves the trigger, the roles of participants, the creator’s response, audience response, and the observable consequence. This prevents a single clipped remark from being treated as an entire social event. The current coding system distinguishes, among other categories, emotional support, personalized attention, paid partiality, emotional labor, surface display shifts, paid intimacy, relational boundary renegotiation, fan identity, moderator defense, ordinary-viewer defense, exclusion, monetization tiers, private-domain funneling, organizational infrastructure, free fan labor, aesthetic labor, sexual harassment, sexualized service demands, and boundary creep.

Evidence is graded by source type. Level A consists of direct material: screenshots, livestream recordings, platform interfaces, and public account pages. Level B consists of an interviewee’s first-hand experience; Level C consists of an interviewee’s report about another person. Uncorroborated industry hearsay is treated as a lead rather than a finding. This hierarchy matters because the market mixes public marketing, private transactions, community slang, and highly variable practices. The analysis therefore avoids converting an anecdote into an industry statistic.

The focal case is treated differently from the comparators. Streamer W is the only account subject to continuing opportunity-based live observation. The project does not schedule fixed watch shifts because the behaviors of interest — a paying customer appearing, a conflict erupting, a moderator intervening, or a gift coinciding with a display shift — are probabilistic. Observation continues when high-information episodes occur and can stop when additional material no longer changes the major mechanisms. The other accounts are analyzed only for what they make publicly visible: how they sell, recruit, route customers, organize back-end labor, structure membership, and construct a marketable persona. This division avoids pretending that every account has been ethnographically observed.

The single deep case is intentional. The strongest behavioral claims in this article concern mechanisms directly observable in the focal ecology: emotional display switching, movement from public content to private ordering, member and audience governance, and organized fan participation. Comparative accounts are used to test whether the surrounding infrastructure is idiosyncratic. They show studios, guilds, MCNs, service contacts, membership groups, and private routing in multiple forms, while also supplying counterexamples: several smaller creators remain closer to individual operations and do not publicly display a studio structure.

One exploratory interview is retained as a supplementary relational case. The interviewee is described only as a female university student who had purchased gaming-companion services. No name, school, location, contact information, or relation to the researcher is disclosed. Her account is not used to establish prevalence. It is used where it provides first-person language for mechanisms otherwise difficult to infer from public behavior: the desire for “favoritism,” the coexistence of friendship and payment, and worker-side boundary pressures.

Ethically, the paper analyzes public-facing commercial behavior and anonymized private-domain materials while removing personal contact details. The focal streamer is pseudonymized in the manuscript even though the source account is public, because the research question concerns mechanisms rather than exposure of an individual. The analysis does not infer hidden motives, diagnose personality, or treat a public “relationship” tag as proof of an offline romantic relationship. When the paper describes a “persona,” it means a patterned public-facing presentation — not the creator’s authentic or inauthentic self.

The design cannot establish how common each mechanism is across China. It can instead connect behaviors that prevalence studies often separate: a phrase in a game, a gift in a livestream, a membership tier, a group announcement, a moderator action, and a private order channel. The aim is mechanism tracing within a bounded digital ecology, followed by cautious comparison across public accounts.

4. Someone Who Is Always on Your Side

The gaming-companion economy is easiest to misunderstand when it is described as the sale of “emotional value.” The phrase is broad enough to explain almost anything. A good joke has emotional value. Winning a ranked match has emotional value. So does relief from loneliness. The more useful task is to identify what is actually being produced.

The exploratory consumer interview in this project gives one answer: the customer can purchase alignment.

The interviewee described an expectation that if other teammates criticized her, the companion would take her side. She did not frame this simply as politeness. She called the feeling a form of companionship “belonging to one person” and described it as “favoritism.” [Field evidence E021.] This is the empirical starting point for the concept of paid partiality.

Paid partiality is not identical to unconditional agreement. A competent companion may still coach, correct, or refuse a request. The key is the relational default. In an uncertain social situation, who is presumed to be “ours”? Who is protected from embarrassment? Who gets the benefit of the doubt? Who receives the first explanation, the softer tone, the queue priority, or the private access?

The broader account sample shows that platforms already price several adjacent forms of priority. Streamer W’s membership page offered a private member group, member-only space, one-to-one question access, priority service, paid connection, exclusive expressions, a member badge, and other benefits. [Field evidence E014.] Other sampled accounts used 680- or 980-diamond membership tiers, private groups, one-to-one teaching, “follow-car” access, and service contacts. [Field evidence E028–E029, E041, E043, E045.] These benefits are not equivalent forms of intimacy. What they share is a mechanism: payment changes the channel, speed, visibility, or exclusivity of access.

This architecture matters because attention is scarce. A creator cannot treat every viewer as equally close. Platform features therefore make differential proximity legible: membership badges identify status; gifts can interrupt the room; private groups reduce the audience; one-to-one features create narrower channels; customer-service contacts move transactions away from the public platform. What appears emotionally as “closer” is also organized technically as unequal access.

Livestream-gifting research makes the surrounding economic logic explicit. Cao et al. (2022) found that verbal intimacy and virtual physical intimacy were associated with users’ shift toward more expensive virtual gifts. Zhang and Liu (2024) similarly found that streamer attractiveness, expertise, and parasocial interaction were positively associated with gift-giving intention. These studies are not about game companions specifically, and they do not prove that intimacy mechanically causes spending. They do show that relational cues and economic differentiation coexist in the surrounding livestream economy.

Gaming companionship adds a sharper element: the customer and provider may enter the same competitive situation. The companion can demonstrate loyalty through play. Protection is not merely said; it can be enacted by following the customer’s character, rescuing them, taking damage, defending them in voice chat, or attacking the teammate who criticizes them. Wang’s (2025) account of Honor of Kings shows that cooperative action itself produces “we-ness.” When the cooperative partner is paid, the relational meaning of “we” becomes economically charged.

This produces a useful distinction between attention and partiality. Attention means that the provider notices the customer. Partiality means that the provider is expected to lean toward the customer. The first can be distributed widely. The second creates a boundary.

The focal case offers an indirect illustration of boundary-making through audience reaction. In one livestream dispute, a moderator defended the streamer’s decision to play with a weaker player by writing, “The streamer is willing to carry [them], what business is it of yours?” A second, apparently ordinary viewer attacked the critic’s motives, and the moderator later removed the dissenter. [Field evidence E010–E013.] The episode does not establish that the protected player had paid, nor does it show unconditional defense of customers. It shows something narrower: a creator-centered room can quickly sort participants into insiders whose presence is protected and outsiders whose criticism is treated as intrusive.

This is where paid partiality becomes a bridge hypothesis rather than a demonstrated lifecycle. It begins as a service expectation — someone is on your side — while creator communities can independently develop their own forms of alignment and boundary enforcement. Future work should test when, if ever, the first process contributes to the second.

The concept is deliberately narrower than “emotional consumption.” It can be observed. Future research could code situations in which a customer and stranger make similar mistakes, then compare the provider’s response; it could ask customers whether they expect correction or public defense when they are clearly wrong; it could measure whether being “on my side” predicts repeat purchase separately from competence, entertainment, and generic sociability. If the concept survives those tests, it may explain why some companionship services remain valuable even when the customer could find technically superior teammates for free.

The product, in other words, may be less “someone who plays with me” than “someone whose default position is next to me.”
