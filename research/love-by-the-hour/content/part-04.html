5. A Person With a Back Office

The most misleading image of the gaming-companion economy is a single person sitting in front of a phone, selling one hour of personality at a time.

The public accounts in this study repeatedly reveal something larger.

Streamer W’s own ecosystem is the clearest example. A group announcement directed viewers who felt strong “immersion” after watching the streamer’s videos toward the streamer’s private messaging account or an official account connected to an affiliated club. [Field evidence E025.] Another archived notice from the affiliated club explained that when daytime customer service was unavailable, users seeking orders at night could contact duty managers, who would add them to an order group. [Field evidence E027.] The infrastructure visible in only a few screenshots already includes a front-stage creator, a branded club, customer service, duty managers, private contacts, member groups, and an order group.

That chain changes the interpretation of “emotional labor.” The warm voice belongs to an individual. The commercial system around the voice does not.

Comparable structures appear across the wider sample. One Honor of Kings creator linked a “real offline studio” from the profile, and the associated WeChat page identified itself explicitly as the creator’s studio. [Field evidence E030–E031.] Another large creator listed separate contacts for “peak-hour package/follow-car” services, studio order problems, and business cooperation. [E032.] Another routed users toward “our own rank-boosting customer service,” a “follow-car wall,” and esports clubs. [E033.] A further account linked an “own studio,” peak-hour follow-car companionship, and a national-server team. [E034.] Other accounts combined studios with VIP groups, product storefronts, MCNs, or clubs. [E035–E042.]

The pattern also appears below celebrity-sized accounts. A micro-streamer with only 3,646 followers publicly linked a major Honor of Kings guild, had 34 membership joins and three group chats, and advertised reward thresholds tied to fan levels. [Field evidence E047.] Two accounts with roughly 50,000 to 70,000 followers belonged to a voice-performance ecology with explicit host/voice-actor examinations, a “hall,” a leader, and internal feedback channels. [E039–E040.] These cases show that formal organization can appear at small scale, although two of them come from the same voice-performance system and should not be treated as independent prevalence evidence.

At the same time, several counterexamples matter. A 47,000-follower technical streamer showed personal business contact, memberships, and groups but no visible studio or guild. [E038.] A 129,000-follower account explicitly branding itself as a “gentle streamer” and offering game companionship routed users toward the creator’s own contact and membership spaces, again without a visible studio. [E043.] An 85,000-follower League of Legends creator emphasized high-ranked credentials and personal maintenance/review contact rather than a formal organization. [E044.]

The conclusion is more precise than “every streamer has a studio.” Commercialized gaming relationships can rely on very different organizational infrastructures: a studio, guild, MCN, club, customer-service team, voice-performance hall, private group, or simply the creator’s own messaging account. The relevant unit of analysis is therefore often the creator-plus-infrastructure, while some creators remain comparatively individual operations.

Zhao’s (2023) concept of fragmented control helps explain why this matters. Chinese platform game work is governed by multiple intermediaries rather than a single platform. Guilds and studios can organize labor, distribute opportunities, maintain communities, and mediate customers. The current sample extends that insight into the consumer-facing side of the business. Intermediaries do not merely control workers. They also manufacture continuity around a persona.

This is particularly important for intimacy. A buyer may experience warmth as personal — “he remembers me,” “she talks to me,” “this is our group” — while the conditions enabling that experience are organizational. A manager answers at night. A customer-service account routes the buyer. A group preserves contact between streams. A membership badge identifies status. A moderator protects the interactional order. A studio handles service problems so the creator does not need to.

The product can therefore feel individual while being produced collectively.

That observation has wider implications for creator-economy research. Scholarship often treats authenticity and persona as properties of an individual creator. Yet some creator businesses resemble service organizations whose emotional front stage is concentrated in one recognizable person. The back office may never enter the audience’s emotional story even when it helps sustain access, continuity, and monetization.

For Streamer W, this distributed production is unusually visible. A person can be marketed as gentle, accessible, family-oriented, technically competent, and emotionally responsive. Behind that image sits a system able to route demand, manage orders, hold private groups, staff different times of day, and preserve the consumer relationship after the livestream ends.

A person with a back office is still a person. That is precisely why the arrangement works.

6. Selling Tenderness

Streamer W’s public persona is built from several ingredients that are individually ordinary and commercially powerful in combination.

The account displays gaming competence. It also presents family scenes: buying phones for parents as a surprise, eating at home, and rural or semi-rural visual environments. [Field evidence E008–E009.] The profile language is emotionally soft. After a viewer gift in the archived livestream, the creator’s voice also became gentle and personalized. Taken together, the public material combines competence, domestic responsibility, recognizability, and softness.

It would be a mistake to claim that this persona was deliberately engineered to match a single female fantasy. The evidence cannot establish the creator’s intention or how every viewer interprets the content. What can be said is that these recurring elements construct a commercially legible male persona. They make technical skill easier to attach to character.

The seven-minute archived livestream is valuable because it exposes how flexible that character can be.

For several minutes, Streamer W insulted other teammates. The language escalated from “three useless players” and “three noobs” to telling someone to “find a building and jump” and to “restart,” a gaming insult used here in a death-directed sense. He mocked intelligence, appearance, and competence and compared a player unfavorably with a village dog. [Field evidence E001–E004.]

At approximately 4:13 in the archived sequence, a viewer gift arrived. The streamer’s tone changed. He thanked “Xibao” warmly for a virtual gift, repeating the name and praise in a softer register. [E005.]

The analytical temptation is obvious: label the first voice authentic and the second fake. The evidence cannot support that distinction. A person can be genuinely angry at teammates and genuinely grateful to a supporter within the same minute. Emotional labor does not require emotional falsity. What the sequence demonstrates is a sharp, relationship-specific change in public display, with the monetized interaction receiving a markedly different register.

Hochschild’s framework is useful here because it shifts attention away from sincerity. The research question becomes: which feeling is appropriate to display to whom? Yuan and Shao’s (2026) “commercial feeling rules” point in the same direction. Chen and Chen’s (2025) work on commodified voice shows how tone itself can become part of the product. In the focal episode, money does not create the voice from nothing. It changes the interactional context in which the voice is deployed.

The contrast also reveals why “persona collapse” is too crude. Online creators operate across multiple relational zones: teammates, paying customers, viewers, moderators, family, critics, business partners, and private groups. The public persona is therefore better understood as a repertoire of displays whose compatibility matters commercially. Risk appears when audiences expect warmth to travel across zones in which the creator does not perform it.

That expectation helps make controversy possible. If a creator is followed for aggression, aggression confirms the brand. If a creator is followed for warmth, cruelty toward strangers can create tension. Audiences may minimize the event, reframe the target as deserving, attack the critic, distance themselves, or leave. The later moderation episode shows that some members of Streamer W’s room were prepared to defend the creator’s choices and punish dissent, but the current evidence does not link that defense to the earlier abusive sequence.

This gap is analytically productive. The paper does not need to prove a psychological theory of “hypocrisy.” It can study the social work required to keep a persona usable after contradictory behavior becomes visible.

The focal case therefore contributes a more concrete way to study commercial persona without deciding whether the streamer is “authentic.” The question is how different public selves are distributed across relationships, and how a creator-centered community handles the contradictions among them.

To a stranger in the match, the streamer can be vicious.

To the person who just sent the gift, he can be tender.

Both happen in public.
