YANTIE LUN / 力耕第二

Li Geng — Farming

After the opening clash over monopoly and war finance, the second chapter asks what really makes a country resilient: grain and labor, public reserves, commercial exchange, or the ability to turn one region's surplus into another region's supply.

02 / LI GENG

The complete second chapter. The argument shifts from why the state needs revenue to what kind of economy can survive scarcity.← READING ROOM

Li Geng begins where Ben Yi leaves off. Once the official side has defended the state's revenue machinery, it has to explain what accumulated goods and money are actually for. The answer is broader than war. Reserves are presented as a technology of survival: they feed soldiers, cushion famine and move resources from times and places of abundance toward shortage.

1. The official case for reserves is a case for shock absorption.

PRIMARY TEXT / 力耕第二RESERVES AND RELIEF
大夫曰：「王者塞天財，禁關市，執準守時，以輕重御民。豐年歲登，則儲積以備乏絕；凶年惡歲，則行幣物；流有餘而調不足也。昔禹水湯旱，百姓匱乏，或相假以接衣食。禹以歷山之金，湯以莊山之銅，鑄幣以贖其民，而天下稱仁。往者財用不足，戰士或不得祿，而山東被災，齊、趙大飢，賴均輸之畜，倉廩之積，戰士以奉，飢民以賑。故均輸之物，府庫之財，非所以賈萬民而專奉兵師之用，亦所以賑困乏而備水旱之災也。」
The official argues that a ruler should control strategic resources, markets and timing so that abundance can be carried across periods of scarcity. In good harvest years the state stores supplies against later shortages; in bad years it releases money and goods, moving surplus toward deficit. He invokes the ancient stories of Yu and Tang using metal and coinage to rescue people during flood and drought.

He then makes the claim contemporary. In recent years, he says, fiscal resources were strained, soldiers sometimes lacked pay, and disasters struck the eastern regions while Qi and Zhao suffered famine. Stored goods from the junshu system and accumulated grain and treasury resources supported both soldiers and famine relief. These reserves, he insists, were not created only to trade with the population or finance armies. They were also meant to rescue people in distress and prepare for flood and drought.
Chinese Text Project ↗Translation: Galok / working translation

This is a substantial expansion of the official position. In Ben Yi, the revenue system is defended because the frontier costs money. Here the same machinery is defended as a buffer stock. The state stores during abundance and releases during shortage. Fiscal capacity becomes insurance against volatility.

2. The Literati answer with a different theory of resilience.

PRIMARY TEXTAGRICULTURE AS THE BUFFER
文學曰：「古者，什一而稅，澤梁以時入而無禁，黎民咸被南畝而不失其務。故三年耕而餘一年之蓄，九年耕有三年之蓄。此禹、湯所以備水旱而安百姓也。草萊不辟，田疇不治，雖擅山海之財，通百末之利，猶不能贍也。是以古者尚力務本而種樹繁，躬耕趣時而衣食足，雖累凶年而人不病也。故衣食者民之本，稼穡者民之務也。二者修，則國富而民安也。《詩》云：『百室盈止，婦子寧止。』也。」
The Literati say the older answer to disaster was simpler: moderate taxation, timely access to natural resources and a population kept steadily at agricultural work. A well-run farming society, they argue, should accumulate one year's reserve after three years and three years' reserve after nine. That is how Yu and Tang prepared for flood and drought.

If fields lie uncultivated, no amount of wealth from mountains, seas or secondary trades can make the population secure. The ancient ideal therefore honored labor and the “root,” planted extensively, followed the seasons and produced enough clothing and food to withstand repeated bad years. Food and clothing are the people's foundation; cultivation is their central work. If those two are maintained, the state is rich and the people are at peace.
Wikisource / Volume I ↗Translation: Galok / working translation

The dispute is now about where resilience should live. The official wants resilience in public stocks, fiscal instruments and the capacity to redistribute. The Literati want resilience embedded in household production and agricultural surplus before the crisis begins. Both sides value reserves; they disagree over who should build them and through what economic structure.

3. The official refuses to make agriculture the only road to wealth.

PRIMARY TEXTMANY ROADS TO WEALTH
大夫曰：「賢聖治家非一寶，富國非一道。昔管仲以權譎霸，而紀氏以強本亡。使治家養生必於農，則舜不甄陶而伊尹不為庖。故善為國者，天下之下我高，天下之輕我重，以末易其本，以虛蕩其實。今山澤之財，均輸之藏，所以御輕重而役諸侯也。汝、漢之金，纖微之貢，所以誘外國而釣胡、羌之寶也。夫中國一端之縵，得匈奴累金之物，而損敵國之用。是以騾驢駱駝，銜尾入塞，驒騱騵馬，盡為我畜，鼲貂狐貉，采旃文罽，充於內府，而璧玉珊瑚琉璃，咸為國之寶。是則外國之物內流，而利不外泄也。異物內流則國用饒，利不外泄則民用給矣。」
The official rejects the idea that a household or a state has only one source of wealth. Wise government, he says, does not rely on a single treasure, and enriching a country does not follow a single road. If everyone had to live only from farming, famous figures of antiquity could never have worked as potters, cooks or specialists.

Good policy takes advantage of differences in value: what is cheap elsewhere can be made valuable here; secondary goods can be exchanged for primary resources; state stores and the wealth of mountains and marshes can be used to manage relative scarcity and influence other states. Gold and fine goods can draw in the valuables of the Hu and Qiang. A single Chinese bolt of silk may obtain foreign goods worth many times as much. Pack animals, horses, furs, textiles, jade, coral and glass flow inward. To the official, this is evidence that exchange can enrich the state while keeping value from leaking outward.
Chinese Text Project ↗Translation: Galok / working translation

This is one of the clearest passages in the early chapters where the official side defends an active commercial strategy. Wealth can come from specialization, relative prices and exchange across political borders. The state is imagined as an actor capable of exploiting differences in valuation rather than simply collecting agricultural tax.

4. The Literati answer that exchange can make a society poorer in the things that matter.

PRIMARY TEXTLUXURY, DISTANCE AND COST
文學曰：「古者，商通物而不豫，工致牢而不偽。故君子耕稼田魚，其實一也。商則長詐，工則飾罵，內懷窺窬而心不怍，是以薄夫欺而敦夫薄。昔桀女樂充宮室，文繡衣裳，故伊尹高逝游薄，而女樂終廢其國。今騾驢之用，不中牛馬之功，鼲貂旃罽，不益錦綈之實。美玉珊瑚出於昆山，珠璣犀象出於桂林，此距漢萬有餘里。計耕桑之功，資財之費，是一物而售百倍其價也，一揖而中萬鍾之粟也。夫上好珍怪，則淫服下流；貴遠方之物，則貨財外充。是以王者不珍無用以節其民，不愛奇貨以富其國。故理民之道，在於節用尚本，分土井田而已。」
The Literati respond that commerce and craft have legitimate roles only when they move useful goods and produce durable necessities. When profit and ornament dominate, merchants learn deception and artisans learn to embellish appearance. The moral cost spreads from specialists into ordinary behavior.

They then attack long-distance luxury trade directly. Mules and donkeys do not replace the productive work of cattle and horses; exotic furs and patterned textiles do not improve the basic supply of cloth. Jade, coral, pearls, rhinoceros horn and elephant products travel enormous distances and consume labor and resources out of proportion to their usefulness. A single luxury object can absorb the equivalent of vast quantities of grain. When rulers prize rare objects, luxury tastes flow downward and domestic wealth flows outward. Government should therefore restrain useless consumption, value basic production and keep land and agriculture at the center of policy.
Wikisource / Volume I ↗Translation: Galok / working translation

The official sees trade in ratios: exchange something relatively cheap for something relatively valuable. The Literati see trade in opportunity cost: count the labor, transport and grain-equivalent resources consumed in producing and moving prestige goods. They are arguing over the unit in which national wealth should be measured.

5. The chapter ends with a blunt disagreement over cities and labor.

PRIMARY TEXTCITIES, COMMERCE AND WORK
大夫曰：「自京師東西南北，歷山川，經郡國，諸殷富大都，無非街衢五通，商賈之所湊，萬物之所殖者。故聖人因天時，智者因地財，上士取諸人，中士勞其形。長沮、桀溺，無百金之積；跖蹻之徒，無猗頓之富。宛、周、齊、魯，商遍天下。故乃商賈之富，或累萬金，追利乘羨之所致也。富國何必用本農，足民何必井田也？」

文學曰：「洪水滔天，而有禹之績；河水泛濫，而有宣房之功；商紂暴虐，而有孟津之謀；天下煩擾，而有乘羨之富。夫上古至治，民樸而貴本、安愉而寡求。當此之時，道路罕行，市朝生草。故耕不強者無以充虛，織不強者無以掩形。雖有湊會之要，陶、宛之術，無所施其巧。自古及今，不施而得報，不勞而有功者，未之有也。」
The official points to the geography of wealth. Across the empire, the richest great cities sit at crossroads where roads meet, merchants gather and goods multiply. Wise people use timing and place; the highest skill is to draw value from other people's activity, while lesser skill depends on one's own physical labor. Commercial centers such as Wan, Zhou, Qi and Lu accumulated fortunes because merchants pursued opportunities across the realm. Why, he asks, must a rich country depend only on agriculture? Why must a well-supplied population depend on the well-field ideal?

The Literati answer that commercial fortunes arise most spectacularly in disturbed times—the way flood creates the occasion for a Yu, political chaos creates the occasion for exceptional strategists, and disorder creates opportunities for speculative wealth. In a well-ordered ancient society, people were simple, valued basic production and wanted little; roads and markets were quiet because households produced what they needed. No amount of clever positioning changes the underlying rule: from antiquity to the present, no one receives a return without some underlying labor and production having taken place.
Chinese Text Project ↗Translation: Galok / working translation

The chapter ends without agreement because the two sides define wealth differently. The official points to urban concentration, trade networks and the ability to profit from geography and exchange. The Literati insist that every commercial surplus ultimately rests on production and that spectacular market fortunes may be symptoms of disorder rather than measures of social success.

That disagreement prepares Chapter 03. Tong You will move even more directly into geography, cities and circulation. The question will no longer be simply whether trade is useful; it will be whether location itself creates wealth.

WHAT LI GENG ESTABLISHES

The second chapter pits two systems of resilience against each other. One relies on public reserves, trade, relative prices and the ability to move value across time and space. The other relies on agricultural depth, restrained consumption and reserves embedded in production itself. Both want security. They disagree over where security should be built.
