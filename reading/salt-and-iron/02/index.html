YANTIE LUN

Li Geng — Farming

力耕第二

After the opening clash over monopoly and war finance, the second chapter asks what really makes a country resilient: grain and labor, public reserves, commercial exchange, or the ability to turn one region's surplus into another region's supply.

READ CHAPTER ↓

02 / LI GENG

The complete second chapter. The argument shifts from why the state needs revenue to what kind of economy can survive scarcity.← READING ROOM

Li Geng is a tighter chapter than Ben Yi, but the disagreement runs deeper than “agriculture versus commerce.” Both sides are trying to answer the same problem: how does a large society survive bad years, uneven geography and limited resources? They disagree over where resilience should be stored — in households and fields, or in public inventories, trade networks and the state's ability to move value across space.

1. The official begins with a theory of shock absorption.

PRIMARY TEXT / 力耕第二02A / RESERVES
大夫曰：「王者塞天財，禁關市，執準守時，以輕重御民。豐年歲登，則儲積以備乏絕；凶年惡歲，則行幣物；流有餘而調不足也……往者財用不足，戰士或不得祿，而山東被災，齊、趙大飢，賴均輸之畜，倉廩之積，戰士以奉，飢民以賑……亦所以賑困乏而備水旱之災也。」
WORKING TRANSLATION

The official argues that a ruler should control strategic resources, markets and timing so that abundance can be carried into scarcity. In good years the state stores supplies; in bad years it releases money and goods, moving surplus toward deficit. He cites recent disasters in the east and famine in Qi and Zhao, claiming that goods accumulated through junshu, granaries and treasury reserves supported both soldiers and hungry civilians. These stores, he insists, exist for disaster relief as well as military finance.

Chinese Text Project ↗Translation: Galok / working translation
GALOK READING

This is the official side's clearest argument for the state as an insurer. The logic is temporal: collect or buy when resources are abundant, hold inventory, then release it when private households are weakest. The treasury becomes a balance sheet for moving purchasing power through time.

The argument also broadens the purpose of junshu. In Chapter 01 it helps explain frontier finance and transport. Here it is recast as emergency capacity. That rhetorical move matters: an institution becomes harder to abolish when it can claim several public purposes at once.

There is a real analytical question hidden inside the rhetoric. Buffer stocks work only if the state can identify shortage, preserve inventory, transport it to the right place and resist diversion. A reserve is not resilience merely because it exists on paper. Administration determines whether stored capacity becomes usable capacity.

2. The Literati relocate resilience into the household economy.

PRIMARY TEXT02B / FARMING AS INSURANCE
文學曰：「古者，什一而稅，澤梁以時入而無禁，黎民咸被南畝而不失其務。故三年耕而餘一年之蓄，九年耕有三年之蓄……草萊不辟，田疇不治，雖擅山海之財，通百末之利，猶不能贍也……故衣食者民之本，稼穡者民之務也。二者修，則國富而民安也。」
WORKING TRANSLATION

The Literati say that moderate taxation, access to natural resources and steady cultivation once allowed households to build their own reserves: one surplus year after three years of farming, three surplus years after nine. If fields lie uncultivated, they argue, wealth from mountains, seas and secondary trades cannot secure the population. Food and clothing are the people's foundation, and cultivation is their central work.

Wikisource / Volume I ↗Translation: Galok / working translation
GALOK READING

The disagreement is now about the location of the buffer. The official trusts centralized stocks and the power to redistribute. The Literati want households to arrive at the crisis already carrying surplus. In modern language, one side prefers pooled institutional insurance; the other prefers decentralized self-insurance through productive capacity.

The Literati also understand resilience as independence. A household that can feed and clothe itself is less exposed to distant officials, volatile markets and failures of transport. Their ideal has a hidden political property: fewer intermediaries means fewer points at which power can extract from the household.

The weakness is scale. Local self-sufficiency can protect against some shocks and perform badly against geographically concentrated disaster. A region hit by drought may need precisely the cross-regional movement of grain that the official side celebrates. Li Geng therefore sets up a genuine trade-off rather than a simple right answer.

3. The official side rejects a one-source theory of wealth.

PRIMARY TEXT02C / MANY ROADS TO WEALTH
大夫曰：「賢聖治家非一寶，富國非一道……使治家養生必於農，則舜不甄陶而伊尹不為庖。故善為國者，天下之下我高，天下之輕我重，以末易其本，以虛蕩其實。今山澤之財，均輸之藏，所以御輕重而役諸侯也……夫中國一端之縵，得匈奴累金之物，而損敵國之用……是則外國之物內流，而利不外泄也。」
WORKING TRANSLATION

The official rejects the idea that a household or state has only one source of wealth. If everyone had to live only from farming, celebrated figures of antiquity could never have worked as potters, cooks or specialists. Good policy exploits differences in value: exchange secondary goods for primary resources, use state stores to manage relative scarcity and trade desirable Chinese goods for foreign animals, furs, textiles, jade and other valuables. In his account, exchange can pull resources inward while limiting what flows outward.

Chinese Text Project ↗Translation: Galok / working translation
GALOK READING

This is an early defense of specialization and terms of exchange. The official does not ask whether imported goods were physically produced by Chinese labor. He asks whether Chinese output can command more value through trade than through direct domestic use.

The phrase “many roads to wealth” also attacks the Literati's occupational hierarchy. A complex economy can generate surplus through craft skill, location, information and exchange as well as cultivation. Wealth becomes a problem of coordination and relative advantage.

Yet the official account quietly assumes that the state can identify favorable exchange and capture its benefits. That is a demanding assumption. If officials misprice goods, chase prestige imports or internalize gains while socializing transport costs, the apparent trade surplus may look very different from the social return.

4. The critics ask what imported abundance is actually worth.

PRIMARY TEXT02D / LUXURY AND OPPORTUNITY COST
文學曰：「古者商通物而不豫，工致牢而不偽……今騾驢之用，不中牛馬之功，鼲貂旃罽，不益錦綈之實。美玉珊瑚出於昆山，珠璣犀象出於桂林，此距漢萬有餘里。計耕桑之功，資財之費，是一物而售百倍其價也……夫上好珍怪，則淫服下流；貴遠方之物，則貨財外充……理民之道在於節用尚本，分土井田而已。」
WORKING TRANSLATION

The Literati answer that trade and craft once served practical exchange but now encourage fraud and luxury. Exotic animals, furs, jade, coral, pearls and other distant goods may travel enormous distances and absorb labor and resources out of proportion to their practical use. When rulers prize rare objects, elite taste spreads downward and wealth is drawn toward expensive consumption. Good government, they argue, should restrain useless demand and protect the productive base.

Wikisource / Volume I ↗Translation: Galok / working translation
GALOK READING

This reply is stronger when read as an opportunity-cost argument than as a complaint about fancy objects. The critics ask what labor, transport capacity and agricultural output are sacrificed to obtain goods whose social utility is low. A high market price does not automatically prove high social value.

They also connect consumption at the top to production below. Elite demand changes what artisans make, where merchants search for profit and what kinds of goods absorb transport. Consumption is treated as a steering mechanism for the economy.

The obvious problem is that “useful” and “useless” are politically loaded categories. The Literati assume the authority to rank needs from above. Their critique of luxury can reveal real resource costs while also collapsing cultural preference into moral failure.

5. The official points to cities as evidence.

PRIMARY TEXT02E / LOCATION, NETWORKS, PROFIT
大夫曰：「自京師東西南北，歷山川，經郡國，諸殷富大都，無非街衢五通、商賈之所臻、萬物之所殖者。故聖人因天時，智者因地財，上士取諸人，中士勞其形……宛、周、齊、魯，商遍天下，故乃賈之富或累萬金，追利乘羨之所致也。富國何必用本農，足民何必井田也？」
WORKING TRANSLATION

The official asks the reader to look at the empire's prosperous cities. Their wealth, he says, is associated with crossroads, traffic, merchants and the concentration of goods. The wise exploit time, place and human networks; some acquire wealth through information and exchange rather than physical labor. Commercial centers such as Wan, Zhou, Qi and Lu show that a country need not rely on farming alone to become rich.

Chinese Text Project ↗Translation: Galok / working translation
GALOK READING

This is the chapter's most modern-looking observation because it recognizes location as an economic asset. A crossroads lowers search and transport costs, concentrates buyers and sellers, thickens information, and makes specialization viable. Wealth can be produced by being connected.

The official is also redefining labor. The Literati privilege visible physical effort; the official treats coordination, timing and arbitrage as productive intelligence. That disagreement will become even more explicit in Chapter 03, Tong You.

Still, prosperous commercial cities do not prove that every household in them is secure. Aggregate wealth and household resilience can move differently. The official uses visible urban abundance as evidence of a successful economic route; the critics are about to ask how much of that abundance actually reaches ordinary lives.

6. The final reply makes labor the non-negotiable base.

PRIMARY TEXT02F / NO RETURN WITHOUT WORK
文學曰：「洪水滔天而有禹之績，河水泛濫而有宣房之功，商紂暴虐而有孟津之謀，天下煩擾而有乘羨之富。夫上古至治，民樸而貴本，安愉而寡求。當此之時，道路罕行，市朝生草。故耕不強者無以充虛，織不強者無以掩形。雖有湊會之要、陶朱之術，無所施其巧。自古及今，不施而得報，不勞而有功者，未之有也。」
WORKING TRANSLATION

The Literati reply that extraordinary fortunes often arise in troubled circumstances, just as extraordinary political achievements arise from flood or disorder. In a well-ordered age, they say, people live simply and demand less. Farming fills the stomach and weaving covers the body; commercial cleverness has little room to operate when basic production is secure. From antiquity to the present, they conclude, there has never been reward without contribution or achievement without labor.

Wikisource / Volume I ↗Translation: Galok / working translation
GALOK READING

The chapter ends by attacking the social legitimacy of commercial profit. The critics suspect that large gains from circulation are often rents created by disorder, scarcity or unequal access rather than new production. Their moral vocabulary points toward a distributional question: who did the work, and who captured the surplus?

That is a sharper claim than “merchants are bad.” It distinguishes productive contribution from the ability to occupy a profitable position. Yet the line is hard to draw. Logistics, information and risk-bearing can create value even when they do not look like farming or weaving.

Li Geng therefore leaves us with two rival definitions of a rich society. The official sees resilience in diversified sources of wealth, circulation and the capacity to reallocate. The Literati see resilience in productive households, low dependence and restrained demand. Each side identifies a real failure mode in the other's model.

CHAPTER 02 / GALOK TAKE

The deepest dispute in Li Geng is about where security comes from. Central reserves can move resources across time and space; household production can reduce dependence on those systems. Trade can turn geography into wealth; it can also redirect labor toward goods whose benefits are concentrated. The chapter is valuable because both models contain a source of resilience and a source of fragility.
