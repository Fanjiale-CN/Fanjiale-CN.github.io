01 / BEN YI

This page covers the complete first chapter rather than splitting it into separate thematic notes.← READING ROOM

Ben Yi works best when it is read as a sequence of collisions, not as one block of “Confucians versus state monopoly.” The speakers repeatedly change the level of the argument: household hardship, moral incentives, frontier finance, war strategy, commerce, and finally the machinery of junshu and pingzhun. This edition therefore keeps the whole chapter on one page while breaking the classical text into smaller exchanges. Each passage is followed immediately by a working translation and a Galok reading.

1. The court begins with a diagnostic question.

PRIMARY TEXT / 本議第一01A / THE QUESTION
惟始元六年，有詔書使丞相、御史與所舉賢良、文學語，問民間所疾苦。
WORKING TRANSLATION

In the sixth year of Shiyuan, an imperial order instructed the chancellor and the imperial secretary to confer with the recommended Worthies and Literati and ask what hardships the people were suffering.

Chinese Text Project ↗Translation: Galok / working translation
GALOK READING

The first sentence sets the burden of proof for everything that follows. The conference is introduced through 疾苦 — hardship — so every fiscal institution will eventually have to be defended in terms of what it does to people outside the court.

That does not make the text a survey of public opinion. Huan Kuan shaped the surviving work after the conference, and the scene has a literary architecture. Still, the choice to open with hardship matters. It tells the reader that revenue is never allowed to remain an accounting question. The state may need money, but the book keeps asking what kinds of costs become invisible when the treasury is the only ledger.

2. “Competing with the people for profit” is a theory of government.

PRIMARY TEXT01B / THE FIRST CHARGE
文學對曰：「竊聞治人之道，防淫佚之原，廣道德之端，抑末利而開仁義，毋示以利，然後教化可興，而風俗可移也。今郡國有鹽、鐵、酒榷、均輸，與民爭利。散敦厚之樸，成貪鄙之化。是以百姓就本者寡，趨末者眾……願罷鹽、鐵、酒榷、均輸，所以進本退末，廣利農業，便也。」
WORKING TRANSLATION

The Literati argue that government should restrain the pursuit of secondary profits and cultivate humane conduct instead of teaching society to chase gain. Salt, iron, the alcohol monopoly and junshu, they say, put the state into competition with the people for profit. In their account, this weakens plain customs, draws people away from agriculture and encourages acquisitiveness. They therefore ask that the policies be abolished so that the “root” occupations can recover.

Chinese Text Project ↗Translation: Galok / working translation
GALOK READING

The phrase 與民爭利 carries more weight than a modern slogan about public and private ownership. The critics are making three claims at once. They allege a material burden, an institutional conflict over profitable channels, and an incentive effect: once the government visibly organizes itself around gain, the rest of society learns the same lesson.

The third layer is easy to miss. The Literati think political economy begins above the marketplace. Official conduct shapes social aspiration. Their “root versus branch” vocabulary therefore mixes production, status and morality. Agriculture is treated as materially foundational and ethically stabilizing; commerce is feared when it becomes the dominant model of success.

That framework is contestable even inside the Han world. Yet dismissing it as simple anti-commerce moralism would also miss the institutional question underneath it: what happens when the regulator, tax collector and market participant begin to occupy the same organization?

3. The official answer is brutally concrete: the frontier costs money.

PRIMARY TEXT01C / FRONTIER FINANCE
大夫曰：「匈奴背叛不臣，數為寇暴於邊鄙，備之則勞中國之士，不備則侵盜不止。先帝哀邊人之久患，苦為虜所係獲也，故修障塞，飭烽燧，屯戍以備之。邊用度不足，故興鹽、鐵，設酒榷，置均輸，蕃貨長財，以佐助邊費……罷之，不便也。」
WORKING TRANSLATION

The senior official points to repeated Xiongnu attacks and the cost of defending the frontier. Barriers, beacon systems and garrisons had to be maintained. Because frontier expenditures were insufficient, he says, the government established salt and iron, the alcohol monopoly and junshu to enlarge revenue and support those costs. Abolition would drain the treasury and leave frontier soldiers without adequate provision.

Chinese Text Project ↗Translation: Galok / working translation
GALOK READING

This is the strongest move available to the official side because it turns an abstract argument about “profit” into a visible public obligation. Fortifications, garrisons, transport, weapons and food have recurring costs. If the military commitment is treated as fixed, the debate quickly becomes a problem of dependable revenue.

Notice the structure of the defense. The official does not first claim that monopoly is inherently superior. He claims that a state with expensive commitments must possess institutions capable of turning economic activity into a reliable fiscal stream. State control is defended as a financing technology.

That distinction matters for the rest of the book. A policy can be justified by a real need and still be badly designed. The existence of a frontier threat does not tell us that every method used to finance the frontier was efficient, fair or administratively clean.

4. The critics attack the spending commitment itself.

PRIMARY TEXT01D / WHAT PRODUCES THE DEFICIT?
文學曰：「孔子曰：『有國有家者，不患寡而患不均，不患貧而患不安。』……王者行仁政，無敵於天下，惡用費哉？」

大夫曰：「匈奴桀黠，擅恣入塞，犯厲中國……又欲罷鹽、鐵、均輸，憂邊用，損武略，無憂邊之心，於其義未便也。」

文學曰：「古者貴以德而賤用兵……今廢道德而任兵革，興師而伐之，屯戍而備之，暴兵露師，以支久長，轉輸糧食無已，使邊境之士飢寒於外，百姓勞苦於內。立鹽、鐵，始張利官以給之，非長策也。」
WORKING TRANSLATION

The Literati argue that good rule should secure order through moral authority and reduce the need for costly force. The official answers that the Xiongnu threat is real and that weakening revenue while maintaining frontier defense is irresponsible. The Literati then push the argument upward: prolonged campaigning, garrisons and endless grain transport are themselves producing the fiscal pressure. Creating profit-seeking offices to finance that cycle, they say, is not a durable strategy.

Chinese Text Project ↗Translation: Galok / working translation
GALOK READING

Here the disagreement becomes a fight over what counts as an “exogenous” cost. The official treats frontier spending as something the state has to absorb. The critics treat much of that spending as a consequence of strategy. If the expenditure is politically chosen, then the revenue system built to support it can no longer be defended simply by pointing to the size of the bill.

This is a useful reading habit beyond this chapter: whenever a government says an institution is necessary because expenditure is high, ask which part of the expenditure is unavoidable and which part is produced by prior policy choices. The text itself is already doing that.

The Literati's alternative — moral transformation reducing external conflict — is far easier to state than to verify. That weakness is part of the debate. Their diagnosis of fiscal feedback is sharper than their proposed security mechanism.

5. Commerce enters as infrastructure, not decoration.

PRIMARY TEXT01E / WHY MARKETS EXIST
大夫曰：「古之立國家者，開本末之途，通有無之用，市朝以一其求，致士民，聚萬貨，農商工師各得所欲，交易而退……故工不出，則農用乏；商不出，則寶貨絕。農用乏，則穀不殖；寶貨絕，則財用匱。故鹽、鐵、均輸，所以通委財而調緩急。罷之，不便也。」
WORKING TRANSLATION

The official argues that a state must open routes between “root” and “branch,” connect places with different supplies and let farmers, merchants and craftsmen obtain what they need through exchange. Without craftsmen, farmers lack tools; without merchants, valuable goods stop circulating. Salt, iron and junshu are therefore defended as mechanisms that move resources and respond to urgent shortages.

Chinese Text Project ↗Translation: Galok / working translation
GALOK READING

This passage is much more sophisticated than a defense of merchants because “merchants create wealth.” It describes economic interdependence. Agriculture needs tools; tools require craft production; specialized production needs circulation. The official side is effectively arguing that the “root” cannot reproduce itself without the “branches” the critics want to subordinate.

That creates a direct problem for the moral hierarchy introduced at the start of the chapter. If farming depends on iron tools and distribution networks, then commerce and craft cannot be dismissed as merely parasitic after-effects of agricultural surplus. They are inputs into agricultural productivity itself.

The official then uses that interdependence to justify state participation. This move still requires scrutiny. Showing that circulation is necessary does not automatically show that circulation must be organized by the state. The chapter quietly slides from “markets are indispensable” to “these government institutions are indispensable.” The critics are right to attack that jump.

6. The critics answer with a theory of administrative imitation.

PRIMARY TEXT01F / WHEN THE STATE CHASES GAIN
文學曰：「國有沃野之饒，而民不足於食者，工商盛而本業荒也；有山海之貨，而民不足於財者，不務民用而淫巧眾也……排困市井，防塞利門，而民猶為非也，況上之為利乎？傳曰：『諸侯好利則大夫鄙，大夫鄙則士貪，士貪則庶人盜。』是開利孔，為民罪梯也。」
WORKING TRANSLATION

The Literati say that fertile land can coexist with hunger when agriculture is neglected, and natural wealth can coexist with poverty when labor is drawn toward useless luxury production. Even when rulers restrain commercial temptations, people may still pursue gain; if the rulers themselves organize around profit, they argue, the effect is stronger. Greed cascades downward through the political order.

Chinese Text Project ↗Translation: Galok / working translation
GALOK READING

The central claim here is imitation. The critics imagine the bureaucracy as a price signal for social status: when officials behave like merchants, commercial accumulation becomes politically prestigious. Their fear is less “trade exists” than “profit becomes the organizing value of the state.”

This is also where their argument risks becoming too sweeping. Hunger can coexist with rich commerce for many reasons; the text quickly attributes the mismatch to moral corruption and occupational drift. That is a hypothesis, not a demonstrated causal chain. A fuller historical reading has to separate rhetoric from evidence.

Still, the institutional instinct is worth preserving: incentive problems can begin at the top. A system that rewards officials for raising revenue may change how they interpret every transaction below them. The final exchange of Ben Yi gives the critics a concrete place to test that claim.

7. Junshu and pingzhun are presented as coordination systems.

PRIMARY TEXT01G / THE OFFICIAL DESIGN
大夫曰：「往者，郡國諸侯，各以其物貢輸，往來煩雜，物多苦惡，或不償其費，故郡置輸官，以相給運，而便遠方之貢，故曰均輸。開委府于京，以籠貨物，賤即買，貴即賣……故曰平準。平準則民不失職，均輸則民齊勞逸，故平準、均輸，所以平萬物而便百姓，非開利孔，為民罪梯者也。」
WORKING TRANSLATION

The official explains that local tribute had once been costly and cumbersome to transport. Transport offices were therefore established to coordinate deliveries and reduce the burden of distant tribute; this was called junshu. Warehouses at the capital could buy when goods were cheap and sell when they were expensive; this was called pingzhun. In the official account, the first equalizes transport burdens and the second stabilizes prices, reducing the opportunities of merchants to profit from extreme differences.

Chinese Text Project ↗Translation: Galok / working translation
GALOK READING

This is the chapter's clearest piece of institutional design. Junshu addresses spatial mismatch: goods, tribute obligations and transport costs are distributed unevenly across the empire. Pingzhun addresses temporal price variation: the state buys into weakness and sells into scarcity.

Seen from the official side, the state is acting as logistics coordinator, inventory holder and counter-speculator. The promise is elegant because one apparatus appears to solve several problems at once — fiscal extraction, transport waste, shortage and merchant arbitrage.

Elegant design is exactly why the next paragraph matters so much. Policies are implemented by local officials with information advantages, coercive power and their own incentives. The critics stop arguing about abstract morality and attack the mechanism at the point where administrative discretion touches households.

8. The final argument is about implementation failure.

PRIMARY TEXT01H / THE CRITICS' FIELD REPORT
文學曰：「古者之賦稅於民也，因其所工，不求所拙，農人納其獲，女工效其功。今釋其所有，責其所無，百姓賤賣貨物，以便上求……縣官猥發，闔門擅市，則萬物並收。萬物並收，則物騰躍；騰躍，則商賈侔利。自市，則吏容姦。豪吏富商積貨儲物以待其急，輕賈姦吏收賤以取貴，未見準之平也。蓋古之均輸，所以齊勞逸而便貢輸，非以為利而賈萬物也。」
WORKING TRANSLATION

The Literati say older taxation took what households were good at producing instead of demanding what they lacked. Under the new system, people may have to sell their own goods cheaply in order to obtain whatever officials require. Local administrations can order cloth production, delay transactions and effectively become counterparties to the population. When government buying expands suddenly, prices rise; merchants profit from the shortage; officials gain room for manipulation; wealthy traders and corrupt officials can accumulate stock and buy cheap to sell dear. The critics therefore say they have not seen the promised “equal transport” or “price stabilization” in practice.

Chinese Text Project ↗Translation: Galok / working translation
GALOK READING

This ending changes the character of the whole chapter. The dispute began with an ideological accusation and ends with a principal-agent problem. The critics describe coercive procurement, informational asymmetry, opportunistic local officials and traders who adapt around the policy. Their strongest case is empirical in form: the institution's stated objective and its observed operation diverge.

The text also shows how intervention can generate the very arbitrage it was designed to suppress. Large official purchases move prices; predictable official urgency creates opportunities for inventory holders; administrators controlling timing and access may capture part of the spread. The state has more market power, but it also creates more valuable points of discretion.

That does not prove every local office behaved this way. The passage is an adversarial account from one side of the debate. Yet it gives us a research program for the rest of the book: keep separating policy objective, administrative mechanism and experienced outcome. Ben Yi has already shown why all three have to be read together.

CHAPTER 01 / GALOK TAKE

Ben Yi is strongest when read as a sequence of nested questions. Does the state need revenue? What produced the spending need? Are markets necessary? Does necessity imply state operation? Can a sound design survive implementation? The chapter refuses to let a single answer settle all five.
