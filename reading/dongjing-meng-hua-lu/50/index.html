50 / 正月十六日

Four nodes: city open, emperor watching, food and lanterns, signal and blackout.↗ THE SIGNAL↗ TEXT DETECTIVE

1. The emperor shows himself.

PRIMARY TEXT50A / XUANDE GATE / GOLDEN PHOENIX
十六日車駕不出，自進早訖，登門樂作，捲簾，御座臨軒，宣萬姓。先到門下者，猶得瞻見天表，小帽紅袍，獨卓子。左右近侍，簾外傘扇執事之人。須臾下簾，則樂作，縱萬姓遊賞。兩朵樓相對：左樓相對，鄆王以次彩棚幕次；右樓相對，蔡太師以次執政戚里幕次。時復自樓上有金鳳飛下諸幕次，宣賜不輟。諸幕次中，家妓競奏新聲，與山棚露台上下，樂聲鼎沸。西朵樓下，開封尹彈壓幕次，羅列罪人滿前，時複決遣，以警愚民。樓上時傳口敕，特令放罪。
WORKING TRANSLATION

On the sixteenth the imperial carriage did not go out. After the morning meal, music played at the gate, the curtain rolled up, the imperial seat faced the balcony, and the ten thousand households were proclaimed. Those who arrived first could still glimpse the imperial face — small cap, red robe, alone at a table. Attendants stood on either side with canopies and fans outside the curtain. In a moment the curtain came down, music played, and the ten thousand households were free to roam and enjoy. The two turret towers faced each other: on the left, colored pavilions and tents in the sequence of Prince Yun and the others; on the right, those of Grand Preceptor Cai and the executive relatives. From time to time a golden phoenix flew down from the tower to the various pavilions, bestowing gifts without cease. In the pavilions, household singers competed with new melodies, and with the mountain frames and platforms above and below, the music boiled. Below the western turret the Kaifeng prefect kept order over his pavilion, criminals arrayed before him, sentences passed from time to time to warn the ignorant. From the tower verbal decrees were sometimes relayed, specially pardoning offenders.

GALOK READING

The emperor shows himself — the only time in the festival calendar. The curtain rises, the people see his face, and then the curtain falls and the festival is released. The golden phoenix is a suspended messenger figure; the research lock notes that comparable mechanisms are independently attested but the exact rigging is unknown. The criminal justice display beside the festival is not commented on by Meng — he simply records it as part of the scene.

2. The signal, and the blackout.

PRIMARY TEXT50B / THIRD WATCH / ALL LAMPS OUT
於是華燈寶炬，月色花光，霏霧融融，動燭遠近。至三鼓，樓上以小紅紗燈球緣索而至半空，都人皆知車駕還內矣。須臾聞樓外擊鞭之聲，則山樓上下，燈燭數十萬盞，一時滅矣。
WORKING TRANSLATION

Then the splendid lamps and precious torches, the moonlight and flower-light, mingled in a soft haze, their glow reaching far and near. At the third watch, from the tower a small red gauze lantern ball descended along a rope to mid-air, and the people all knew the carriage was returning to the palace. In a moment they heard the crack of whips outside the tower, and on the mountain and the tower, above and below, hundreds of thousands of lamps went out all at once.

GALOK READING

The blackout sequence is the page’s center: SIGNAL (the lantern ball descends) → RECOGNITION (the people know) → SOUND (the whip cracks) → BLACKOUT (all lamps out at once). The quantity 數十萬盞 is Meng’s claim, marked as such. The obedience of the city to a single descending light is the festival’s most compressed display of power.

3. The festival fragments.

PRIMARY TEXT50C / TEMPLES / LANES / SHADOW PLAYS / PRICES
於是貴家車馬，自內前鱗切，悉南去游相國寺。寺之大殿，前設樂棚，諸軍作樂，兩廊有詩牌燈云：「天碧銀河欲下來，月華如水照樓台。」並「火樹銀花合，星橋鐵鎖開」之詩。其燈以木牌為之，雕鏤成字，以紗絹冪之於內，密燃其燈，相次排定，亦可愛賞。資聖閣前安頓佛牙，設以水燈，皆係宰執、戚里、貴近佔設看位。最耍鬧：九子母殿及東西塔院，惠林、智海，寶梵，競陳燈燭，光彩爭華，直至達旦。其餘宮觀寺院，皆放萬姓燒香。如開寶、景德大佛寺等處。皆有樂棚，作樂燃燈。惟禁宮觀寺院，不設燈燭矣。次則葆真宮有玉柱玉簾窗隔燈。諸坊巷、馬行，諸香藥鋪席、茶坊酒肆，燈燭各出新奇。就中蓮華王家香舖燈火出群，而又命僧道場打花鈸、弄椎鼓，遊人無不駐足。諸門皆有官中樂棚。萬街千巷，盡皆繁盛浩鬧。每一坊巷口，無樂棚去處，多設小影戲棚子，以防本坊遊人小兒相失，以引聚之。殿前班在禁中右掖門裡，則相對右掖門設一樂棚，放本班家口，登皇城觀看。官中有宣賜茶酒妝粉錢之類。諸營班院於法不得夜遊，各以竹竿出燈球於半空，遠近高低，若飛星然。阡陌縱橫，城不禁。別有深坊小巷，繡額珠簾，巧制新妝，競誇華麗，春情蕩颺，酒興融怡，雅會幽歡，寸陰可惜，景色浩鬧，不覺更闌。寶騎駸駸，香輪轆轆，五陵年少，滿路行歌，萬戶千門，笙簧未徹，市人賣玉梅、夜蛾、蜂兒、雪柳、菩提葉、科頭圓子、拍頭焦𩜼。唯焦𩜼以竹架子出青傘上，裝綴梅紅縷金小燈籠子，架子前後亦設燈籠，敲鼓應拍，團團轉走，謂之「打旋羅」，街巷處處有之。至十九日收燈，五夜城闉不禁，嘗有旨展日。宣和年間，自十二月於酸棗門（二名景龍）門上，如宣德門元夜點照，門下亦置露台，南至寶籙宮，兩邊關撲買賣，晨暉門外設看位一所，前以荊棘圍繞，周回約五七十步，都下賣鵪鶉骨飥兒、圓子、拍、白腸、水晶鱠、科頭細粉、旋炒栗子、銀杏、鹽豉、湯雞、段金橘、橄欖、龍眼、荔枝。諸般市合，團團密擺，準備御前索喚。以至尊有時在看位內，門司、御藥、知省、太尉，悉在簾前，用三五人弟子祗應。籸盆照耀，有同白日。仕女觀者，中貴邀住勸酒一金杯令退。直至上元，謂之「預賞」。惟周待詔瓠羹，貢餘者一百二十文足一個，其精細果別如市店十文者。
WORKING TRANSLATION

Then the carriages of the great houses, packed rim to rim from the palace front, all headed south to Xiangguo Temple. Before the great hall a music pavilion was set up, the military played, and along the corridors were poem-board lanterns reading: The azure sky, the Milky Way about to descend; moonlight like water shining on the towers. And: Fire trees and silver flowers merge; the star bridge and its iron locks open. The boards were carved with the characters, covered inside with gauze, lamps secretly burning within, arranged in sequence — a delight to see. Before the Zisheng Pavilion the Buddha’s tooth was displayed with water lanterns; viewing spots were claimed by the chief councillors, imperial relatives and the favored close to the throne. The liveliest spots: the Nine Mothers Hall and the east and west tower courts, Huilin, Zhihai, Baofan — each competing with lamps and candles, brilliance vying with brilliance, until dawn. All the other temples and monasteries opened for the people to burn incense: the Kaibao and Jingde great Buddha temples all had music pavilions, playing and lighting lamps. Only the Daoist temples were forbidden to set up lamps. Next, the Baozhen Temple had jade-pillar and jade-curtain window lanterns. The lanes and Maxing, the spice shops and tea houses and wine shops — each outdid the others with lamp novelties. Among them the Lianhua Wang spice shop’s lights stood out, with monks performing cymbal-crashing and drum-beating — no passerby failed to stop. Every gate had an official music pavilion. Ten thousand streets and a thousand lanes, all teeming and roaring. At every lane entrance without a music pavilion, small shadow-play booths were set up, to keep the neighborhood’s children from getting lost and to draw them together. The Palace Front guard, inside the Right Yi Gate, set up a music pavilion opposite it for their own families to climb the city wall and watch. The palace bestowed tea, wine, cosmetic powder and money. The military camps were by law not permitted to roam at night; each raised lantern balls on bamboo poles into the air, far and near, high and low, like flying stars. The lanes crisscrossed; the city gates were not closed. And there were deep lanes and small alleys with embroidered door-curtains and pearl bead-screens, cleverly made new costumes, vying in splendor; spring feeling stirring, wine-mood mellow, refined encounters and hidden joys, every moment precious; the scene so teeming that the watches passed unnoticed. Fine horses clip-clopping, fragrant carriage wheels rumbling, the young men of the Five Mausoleums singing full-length down the road, ten thousand households and a thousand gates, the pipes not yet silent; the market people selling jade plum blossoms, night moths, bees, snow willows, bodhi leaves, ke-tou round dumplings, pai-tou fried cakes. The fried cakes alone came out on green umbrellas from bamboo frames, decorated with plum-red and gold-thread small lanterns; lanterns were also set before and behind the frames, with drums beating the rhythm as they spun in circles — called the spinning round — found everywhere in the streets and lanes. Through the nineteenth, when the lanterns were taken in, the city gates were not closed for five nights; an edict had extended the days. In the Xuanhe era, from the twelfth month at the Suanzao Gate (also called Jinglong), lanterns were lit as at Xuande Gate on festival nights; a platform was also set up below the gate, extending south to the Baolu Temple, with guanpu trading on both sides. Outside the Chenhui Gate a viewing stand was set up, enclosed by thorn branches, about fifty to seventy paces around. The capital’s vendors sold quail-shaped guduo dumplings, round dumplings, pai cakes, white sausage, crystal sliced fish, ke-tou fine noodles, roast chestnuts, gingkoes, salted-bean soup with chicken, sectioned golden tangerines, olives, longans, lychees. All kinds of market goods were closely arrayed, ready for the imperial summons. When the emperor himself sometimes appeared at the viewing stand, the gate officers, palace physicians, secretaries and commanders all stood before the curtain, attended by three to five disciples. The hemp-residue braziers blazed, bright as day. Ladies watching were stopped by palace officials who offered a golden cup of wine and sent them back. Up to the Lantern Festival this was called the pre-viewing. Only Supervisor Zhou’s gourd soup: the tribute version cost one hundred twenty cash each, its fine ingredients distinct from the market’s ten-cash version.

GALOK READING

The festival fragments across multiple nodes: the temple grounds (Xiangguo with its poem-board lanterns and Buddha tooth), the commercial lanes (each shop competing with lamp novelties), the neighborhood shadow-play booths (gathering lost children), and the pre-viewing zone at the Suanzao Gate (with its 120-cash premium soup). The signal sequence and the blackout are the page’s temporal center; the fragmentation is its spatial argument. The entry also records who is excluded: the military camps, restricted by law from night movement, raise lantern balls on poles — participating at a distance.

04 / TEXT DETECTIVE

Prices, punctuation, and the limits of the record.

鹽豉湯PUNCTUATION CRUX

Gutenberg splits 鹽豉 / 湯雞 / 段金橘; the correct segmentation is 鹽豉湯 as one item, separate from 雞段 and 金橘. The page follows the corrected reading.

科頭細粉MIXED / VOCABULARY FAMILY

科頭 and 科斗 form a vocabulary family; the exact dish cannot be equated with modern liangfen. The page preserves the transmitted term.

周待詔瓠羹NOMINAL PRICE 120 : 10

The premium gourd-soup costs 120 cash; the market version 10. The text itself says the premium is finer. The page presents the price without modern conversion.

金鳳THE GOLDEN PHOENIX

A suspended messenger figure that descended to deliver gifts.

三鼓THIRD WATCH

The signal for the emperor’s return and the blackout.

小影戲棚SHADOW-PLAY BOOTH

A neighborhood beacon to gather lost children.

城闉不禁GATES OPEN

Festival relaxation was selective: city gates open, barracks restricted.

籸盆ILLUMINATING BRAZIERS

Ground-level fire baskets, not hanging lanterns.

預賞PRE-VIEWING

The pre-festival viewing zone, separate from the main lantern mountain.

03 / THE FESTIVAL FRAGMENTSOne signal, and the city goes dark.

Entry 50 is the densest text in the book. The emperor shows himself; a golden phoenix descends; the festival fragments across temples, shops, neighborhood lanes and shadow-play booths. At the third watch a single red lantern descends, a whip cracks, and every lamp in the mountain goes dark at once. The signal sequence is the page’s center — and the city’s obedience to it is the argument.
