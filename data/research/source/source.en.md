# China, in more than one number — editorial source

- Target: `https://galok.me/data/`
- Language: English
- Research cut-off: 12 August 2026
- Main window: 2000–2025
- Position: an editorial reading of public data and published research. It is not investment advice, and no single statistic is treated as a complete account of political or social life.

## Central claim

China is more than a growth rate. Real GDP grew by about 5% in 2025, while consumer prices were roughly flat, producer prices fell, property development investment contracted, private investment fell and land-sale revenue declined. Productive capacity, public-sector balance sheets, business confidence and household experience can all move in different directions at the same time.

This article asks three questions:

1. Which layer of China does each statistic describe?
2. Why can several accurate datasets tell different stories?
3. What do official institutions, Chinese scholars and market researchers, household and business behaviour, and international institutions choose to emphasise?

## 01 Growth is still present. Prices are weak.

China's real GDP grew by about 5% in 2025. The National Bureau of Statistics said the main annual targets were achieved; the IMF also estimated 5.0% growth and expected a slowdown in 2026.

Annual CPI inflation was approximately zero, while PPI fell 2.6%. Weak prices do not mean that output stopped growing. They do, however, depress the nominal values through which households, companies and local governments experience the economy: wages, sales, profits and the tax base.

General public-budget revenue was RMB 21.60 trillion, down 1.7%. Tax revenue rose 0.8% and corporate income-tax receipts rose 1.0%. Real output and nominal fiscal revenue can therefore diverge.

Economist Huang Yiping describes the longer pattern as "strong supply, weak demand": a powerful production system paired with relatively weak demand and prices. The IMF puts more weight on the property adjustment, local public finance and weak domestic demand as sources of deflationary pressure. The diagnosis overlaps; the policy frame and the weight given to external spillovers differ.

Retain the existing World Bank 2000–2024 explorer for real GDP, CPI, household consumption and gross capital formation. Put four 2025 signals on a common baseline: real GDP +5.0%, CPI about 0%, PPI −2.6%, and public-budget revenue −1.7%. Do not use a dual axis.

## 02 Households: why growth has not passed through to consumption in the same way

Per-capita disposable income reached RMB 43,377 in 2025 and grew by about 5% in real terms. Median disposable income was RMB 36,231 and grew 4.4% in nominal terms. The median being lower than the mean is a reminder that distribution is closer to the experience of the typical household than the average alone.

The World Bank estimates the household saving rate at about 31% of disposable income, well above the OECD average. Chinese scholars have long linked household saving to the social safety net, housing and education risks, income distribution and household balance sheets.

Retail sales rose 3.7%. Trade-in subsidies supported appliances and communications equipment, but policy-driven durable-goods demand is not the same as a broad and lasting recovery in consumer confidence.

China recorded 7.92 million births and 11.31 million deaths in 2025, so the population continued to decline. Population structure changes long-run demand for housing, education, pensions and local public services.

The domestic policy narrative now gives more space to raising incomes, social protection, childcare support and "investing in people." Economists including Huang Yiping and Liu Yuanchun connect low consumption with distribution, safety nets and household balance sheets. This article does not substitute anonymous posts for "public opinion"; it uses comparable behavioural proxies such as saving, consumption, median income, home purchases and job seeking.

The World Bank argues that stronger social protection and better services for rural and migrant households, alongside local-fiscal repair, would release consumption. The IMF argues for shifting demand support more decisively toward household consumption and away from debt-led investment and export dependence.

## 03 Employment: aggregate stability and youth pressure coexist

The urban surveyed unemployment rate averaged about 5.2% in 2025 and 12.67 million urban jobs were added.

In November 2025, unemployment among urban non-student 16–24 year-olds was 16.9%. The rate was 7.2% for ages 25–29 and 3.8% for ages 30–59. These groups face different labour markets.

Employees in surveyed enterprises worked about 48.6 hours a week. Job quantity, age distribution, hours and wage quality cannot be reduced to one unemployment rate.

The youth series was suspended in 2023 and resumed with students excluded. Comparisons across the break must be labelled. The urban survey does not cover the full rural labour market, people who have stopped looking for work or the quality of each job.

## 04 Property and local public finance: two sides of one balance sheet

Property development investment fell 17.2% to RMB 8.279 trillion in 2025. New commercial floor space sold fell 8.7% and sales value fell 12.6%.

Local state-owned land-use-right sale revenue fell 14.7% to RMB 4.152 trillion. National government-fund revenue fell 7%, while expenditure from those funds rose 11.3%.

Local governments account for roughly 85% of national general public-budget expenditure. They carry large responsibilities for education, social security, healthcare and infrastructure, while the decline in land revenue and debt constraints narrow their usable fiscal space.

Policy continued to use local special-purpose bonds, ultra-long special treasury bonds and swaps of existing debt. These measures can reduce near-term refinancing risk. They do not automatically restore the cash flow once supplied by land and property.

The Ministry of Finance stresses faster bond deployment, rising priority social expenditure and overall fiscal stability. Chinese researchers focus more on the broad fiscal stance, how much genuinely new demand remains after debt swaps, and how local growth incentives can be reconciled with public-service obligations. The IMF and World Bank emphasise spillovers from property into local finance, debt restructuring, comprehensive fiscal reporting and reform of local revenue structures.

## 05 Investment: not just how much, but who invests and where

Fixed-asset investment fell 3.8% in 2025. Private fixed-asset investment fell 6.4%; even excluding property, it fell 1.9%.

Investment in information services grew 28.4%, and investment in aerospace equipment manufacturing grew 16.9%. Aggregate weakness and rapid expansion in selected strategic sectors can coexist.

CKGSB's Business Conditions Index is a monthly diffusion survey of private-company managers, with 50 as the dividing line between expansion and contraction. It read 49.4 in January 2025, 46.9 in August, 51.1 in September and 49.8 in December. It is a forward-looking sample, not a census of the entire private economy.

This is a K-shaped investment picture. Property and parts of traditional demand weaken while policy-supported and technology-intensive activity expands. A policy agency may call it structural upgrading; a private firm may see weak orders, profits and credit expectations; an external institute may see questions about industrial support, capacity and trade spillovers. Each reading must identify its object.

## 06 The external account: exports as buffer and source of dispute

The goods-trade surplus reached roughly US$1.19 trillion in 2025. The IMF estimated the current-account surplus at about 3.3% of GDP. A goods surplus and a current-account balance are not the same measure.

Chinese commentary emphasises competitiveness, market diversification and resilience. International institutions give more weight to the real-exchange-rate effect of low inflation, external imbalance and spillovers to trading partners.

High-technology manufacturing, green industries and exports can raise productivity. When domestic demand is weak, the same strengths can also intensify overseas disputes about capacity and subsidy.

## 07 Political economy: what a budget can reveal

This article does not reduce politics to a regime label or a subjective score. It uses auditable budgets, central–local relations, investment direction, jobs and social expenditure to discuss capacity and choices. Motives that cannot be supported by public evidence remain interpretations, not facts.

General public-budget expenditure reached RMB 28.74 trillion in 2025, up 1%. Social security and employment expenditure grew 6.7%, health expenditure 5.7%, and science and technology expenditure 4.8%.

Government-fund expenditure reached RMB 11.29 trillion, up 11.3%. Spending backed by ultra-long special treasury bonds, local special-purpose bonds and special treasury bonds for financial-institution capitalisation totalled RMB 6.19 trillion.

Local governments execute about 85% of general public-budget spending. The centre shapes available space through transfers, debt quotas and earmarked programmes.

Four questions organise the evidence:

1. Capacity: how much debt issuance, transfer and industrial mobilisation can the state organise?
2. Constraint: how do land revenue, local debt, weak prices and the tax base limit it?
3. Priority: how is the balance between technology and industry, infrastructure and household services changing?
4. Feedback: do household confidence, private investment, local execution and external friction validate the policy objective?

Budget data can show resource allocation, central–local division and counter-cyclical capacity. It cannot by itself demonstrate effectiveness or public satisfaction.

## 08 Four vantage points

| Vantage point | Main object | Representative reading | Likely blind spot |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Official institutions | Output, aggregate employment, policy targets, budget execution | Resilience remains; the structure is upgrading; policy space remains | Distribution, lived prices, local variation |
| Chinese scholars and market institutes | Prices, balance sheets, private firms, policy transmission | Strong supply and weak demand; support consumption, social protection and confidence | External spillovers and cross-country comparison |
| Household and business behaviour | Saving, spending, investment, job seeking, home buying | Risk appetite remains low; expectations for income and assets are cautious | Behaviour is not a complete account of subjective opinion |
| IMF, World Bank and external institutes | Rebalancing, debt, productivity, cross-border spillovers | Shift policy toward households, repair property and local finance, reduce export dependence | The detail and path of domestic implementation |

This is not a mechanical exercise in "both sides." Every claim should answer: who is speaking, which indicator is being used, what date applies, and what limits the sample or definition?

## 09 Data boundaries

1. Real GDP and nominal income are not the same measure.
2. A goods-trade surplus and the current account are not the same balance.
3. The youth unemployment series has a method break around 2023.
4. The headline budget deficit excludes some government funds, special-purpose bonds and implicit liabilities.
5. Property and investment growth rates can be revised after censuses, institutional reforms and enforcement reviews.
6. BCI and confidence surveys depend on their samples and questionnaires.
7. Individual social posts cannot represent public opinion; this article uses verifiable behavioural indicators only as proxies.
8. Institutional forecasts are revised, so date and value must travel together.

## 10 Conclusion

At least three Chinas coexist:

- A China of production, in which manufacturing capacity, exports and strategic industries remain strong.
- A China of balance sheets, in which property, local finance and private investment are under pressure.
- A China of household life, in which incomes still grow, but age-specific employment pressure, asset prices and the safety net shape the willingness to spend.

The useful question is not which China is "real." It is whether productive capacity can be converted more reliably into household income, public services and sustainable domestic demand.
